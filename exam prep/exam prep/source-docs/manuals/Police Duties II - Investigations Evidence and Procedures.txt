Background 
In accordance with the strategic direction of the Trinidad and Tobago Police Service, the 
Commissioner of Police has mandated the Police Service Examination Board (PSEB) to 
reform the current examination regime which is in use in the Second Division of the TTPS. 
The following changes have been adopted: 
 
i. a revision of curriculum with the purpose of developing modern, candidate 
friendly syllabi. 
ii. the incorporation of examination components that reflect the demands of the 
current job descriptions of the ranks from Corporal to Inspector in the Second 
Division as well as the new and pressing demands on the modern Police Service. 
iii. the administration of examinations in formats which allow comprehensive 
assessment of required knowledge, skills, and abilities. 
 
In alignment with this mandate, the substantive compo nent of LAW has been replaced 
by an examination titled Police Duties Ⅱ – Investigations, Evidence and Procedures (PD-
Ⅱ). This examination  is aimed at assessing the knowledge and competence of the 
candidates, in effectively executing their duties on the issues that relate to criminal 
conduct and the justice system, and the processes and procedures that are involved in the 
conduct of an investigation.   A detailed syllabus for this subject area is presented to the 
candidates as a subject guide to the examination. It provides a comprehensive, high 
quality and relevant set of content standards by listing the official source of the content 
and summarizing the key points therein. The Police Officer is a major part of enforcing 
the system of rules and regulations created by societies. The objective of the (PD-Ⅱ) 
syllabus, is to engender a sound knowledge and understanding of the lawful concepts 
and principles involved in the more common situations faced by Police Officers. The 
Police Officer who can demonstrate analytical and evaluative skills when faced with 
investigative and evidential dilemmas is best placed to function at the rank for which 
they are being assessed. A highly skilled and knowledgeable Police Officer will be 
prepared to meet the challenges and opportunities of the rapidly changing demands of 
policing in the 21st century.  
 
This syllabus is the product of collaboration between a curriculum developer and an 
experienced member of the police service.  It is designed to promote a more student -
centered approach to instruction and to support self-study in preparation for assessment.

Syllabus underpinnings  
The organization and content of the PD-Ⅱ syllabus is shaped by the nature and structure 
of the subject matter to be learnt.  There is an attempt to recognize the dynamic and 
evolving nature of the policing environment. This means that the subject matter covered 
is reality-based, current and sufficient for its purp ose. The PD-Ⅱ syllabus must respond 
to changes in the strategic direction of the organisation, cohere with new social and 
cultural imperatives, and keep up with legislative and administrative protocols as they 
arise. As such, this PD-Ⅱ syllabus is deemed to be current for the period 2022-2025 and 
will be subject to review thereafter.  
 
However, there are three major curriculum underpinnings that are expected to remain 
constant. These are the constructivist approach to learning, the identification of essential 
outcomes from learning and the importance of English Language skills. 
 
These principles are discussed briefly below. 
The constructivist 
approach to learning 
In constructivism, the  learner uses prior academic 
knowledge as well as personal experience to l earn well. 
The Police Officer should therefore approach learning by 
actively and mindfully engaging with the material; 
putting the information into a context, creating and 
interrogating possible scenarios and seeing that 
knowledge of key terms, strategies,  principles, rules, and 
protocols included in this syllabus can inform solutions to 
real-life problems in policing. Finally, the constructivist 
believes that motivation is a key component in learning 
and therefore the increasing responsibility of the role of 
the Corporal, Sergeant or Inspector should be enough of 
an impetus for a sincere effort at covering the PD-Ⅱ 
syllabus. 
The essential learning 
outcomes  
Excellent problem -solving and communication skills are 
deemed essential outcomes of engaging with the PD-Ⅱ 
syllabus. Police Officers are called, individually and 
collaboratively, to distinguish facts from opinions, ask 
questions, observe, make inferences, and draw 
conclusions. Skills in oral and written communication 
modes are necessary for the police officer to demonstrate 
understanding of facts and relationships, present 
information and instructions with clarity and conciseness 
and demonstrate sensitivity and empathy in behaviour 
and speech where necessary.

The importance of

learning and assessment 
The language skills of reading and writing are very 
important in completing this syllabus and being ready for 
assessment. Reading requires the ability to interpret the 
vocabulary used, extract relevant information (analysis), 
reconstruct the message and respond with reasonable 
judgement. You should be able to read both long and short 
pieces of text intensively; looking for key words and 
phrases and making links with other pieces of information 
which you read before.  Writing demonstrates your ability 
to think logically, organize your thoughts, and use 
appropriate words, terms or jargon.  Good writing allows 
assessors to understand your approach and stay interested 
in your arguments

Assessment forms 
The PD-Ⅱ syllabus is written to be used by all candidates for the three ranks in the Second 
Division, however, the complexity of assessment strategies will increase according to the 
rank being assessed. The questions are not based on the more complex issues that require 
knowledge from any of the specialized units in the Police Service. The summative 
examination for each rank will contain a judicious mix of objective and subjective type 
questions ranging from multiple choice, short answer, and essay to mini-case studies. 
Scheme of the Examination 
Each Second Division  PD-Ⅱ Examination will consist of three Sections. Section 1 will 
consist of multiple choice, true/false or short answer questions based on any topic in the 
entire syllabus. Section 2 will consist of case studies which set out a series of suspicious 
events faced by police officers and which require resolution through the application of 
the relevant law and principles. A case study may be either micro (150-200-word passage) 
or mini (400-500-word passage). Each passage will be followed by a directive instruction 
that require a  structured approach in answering the question . Thus, the candidate’s 
answer should demonstrate that the issues for consideration have been identified, and 
the candidate is aware of the relevant legal principles from cases and statutes, that will 
be applicable to the given situation. An argument should be developed by applying the 
legal rules to the given facts, to arrive at a considered conclusion. In addition to content, 
answers will be assessed  for correct use of the English language, in particular, verb 
agreement, punctuation, spelling and vocabulary use.  
Section 3 will consist of constrained-response questions whose answers will demonstrate 
candidates’ grasp of particulars in named laws and guidelines as it relates to policing and 
the criminal justice system.

Preparation for Examination 
As noted under assessment forms, the examination paper for this subject will consist of 
a judicious mix of objective and subjective type questions ranging from multiple choice, 
true-false, short answer, micro-case studies and questions requiring responses in essay 
format. This section of the syllabus provides information to support excellent responses 
to the requirements of these various types of examination questions. However, tips on 
preparing for examinations generally, are set out first.  
 
Tips on writing examinations 
General Approach 
As the examination approaches it is useful to make adequate preparation by doing the 
following: 
i. Organize your notes reducing them to key points. 
ii. Make master cards/flash cards or index cards for quick revision.  
iii. Select what to revise and prepare a range of answers to possible questions on 
each topic. 
iv. Draw up a time table and work out how much time you have to revise, build 
in potential emergencies. 
v. Start your revision early.

Writing The Examination 
i. Once you ar e seated in the examination room, and have been provided with 
your examination paper and answer booklet, etc. settle yourself and follow all 
instructions given by the invigilator.  
ii. Read the instruction on the examination paper carefully, at least twice.  
iii. Fill out any personal details as required, e.g. examination candidate no. 
iv. Make a preliminary scan of the whole paper to make sure that all expected 
sections are there and your paper is not faulty. 
v. Write out the time that you should start and finish each section so that you can 
complete the whole paper. Be sure to leave a few minutes to review your work 
at the end.  
vi. Complete the compulsory questions as best as possible before moving to the 
section where a choice of questions is presented (Essay-response questions). 
vii. When selecting from non -compulsory questions, read each question through 
at least twice. Compare what seems to be expected, in general for each 
question. Notice how many parts there are to the question. Read the question 
through again very slowly to make sure that you have not misread key words. 
Tick the question/s you will attempt and the one you can do best tick twice. 
Draft brief notes/key terms related to the topic of the questions that you have 
selected. You may realize that a question is not what you thought, and you may 
need to select another. 
 
Completing The Examination 
Towards the end of the examination, use the last minutes to 
i. check that you have attempted all compulsory questions,  
ii. read your answers and correct any obvious errors. 
iii. respond to unfinished questions in note form showing how you would have 
addressed the particular issues if you had had the time. This could gain you 
some valuable marks. 
 
Tips on responding to different question formats. 
 
Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs) 
An MCQ consists of a stem and a number of options. The stem is usually a statement or 
a question but may be numerical data, a graphic or a paragraph that give rise to a 
number of follow-up questions and options. The options are usually four in number 
and the correct option may complete a statement, answer the question or is the end 
result of a manipulation of data.

The following approach to an MCQ should be taken. 
1. Cover up the options and read the entire question carefully, taking note of key 
terms. 
2. Formulate an answer to the question before reading the options. Your first thought 
is usually correct, so avoid the temptation to change answers unless you are 
absolutely certain about the correction.  
3. Look for your choice in the list of options but make s ure and read all the options 
since a well -crafted multiple choice presents false options that are close to the 
correct one.  
4. Pay attention to a possible link between stem and the various options. Sometimes 
a particular option does not appear well-connected to the stem. 
5. If one of the options is ‘All of the above’  it may not always be correct. If you are 
sure two or three of the other options are correct, then ‘All of the above’ is a distinct 
possibility. 
6. Ignore the assumption that if you are not sure choose the C option. Do not pay 
attention to how many A ’s, B ’s, C’s etc., you have already selected, to decide 
whether to change your answer. Always choose your best answer.  
7. If you are not sure about the answer, cross out the options that you know are 
definitely wrong. Mark the question with a ‘?’ and return to it close to the end of 
the section or the examination paper. 
 
True-False Questions 
A true-false question takes the form of a statement that you must judge as being factual 
(T) or not (F). The statement often includes terms which suggest that something is 
absolutely true or false or relatively true or false. Occasionally, the candidate may be 
asked to justify the choice of true or false by citing the relevant basis. 
The following approach should enable you to decide. 
1. Look out for absolute terms (never, always, only, necessary, must, entirely, no, 
every, any etc.). These statements are rarely true. 
2. Look out for relative terms (often, seldom, sometimes, perhaps, generally, 
ordinarily, frequently, etc.). These s tatements make more modest claims that are 
more likely to reflect reality and are thus often true. 
3. Be careful with statements containing the word “and”. All parts or phrases of the 
statement have to be true for you to select “True” as your answer.

Writing The Essay 
An essay has three major parts: introduction, main body and conclusion. 
The Introduction 
A good introduction sets the context for the essay, making it clear what you think the 
problem or question is, how you are going to approach it and how the essay should 
develop. It should contain some comments on your understanding of the title and the 
main issues that you are going to address and why. In other words, outline the main 
points you will be expanding in the main body. The introduction provides the 
examiner/reader with a preview  of the stages through which the essay will develop. It 
usually does not need to be more than a paragraph. 
The Main Body 
The main body of the essay provides the opportunity for you to present explanations and 
arguments with ideas, opinions, and facts. Key points should be presented in a logical 
manner and the linkages between them made explicit. It is vital that key points are 
supported by relevant examples or evidence.  
Each paragraph should raise a central issue aspect or idea and include evidence to 
support it. Each paragraph should be complete within itself but should be linked with 
the preceding and following paragraphs. Two paragraphs may present different ideas or 
sides of an argument but linked to that difference: for example, paragraph 1 may address 
the cause of something and paragraph 2 may explain the effect. Other approaches may 
separate the positive and negative aspects of an argument, or describe a before and after 
situation.  
Continuity across paragraphs should be maintained and to signal to the reader how you 
are moving on by the use of linking words or phrases: such as however, similarly, 
consequently, as a result, on the other hand , etc. The main body should present a balanced 
view and all aspect of the title are given adequate coverage.

The Conclusion 
A good conclusion signals to the reader that the essay has ended. It should show the 
reader/examiner how the essay answered what was required of you. Ensure that all the 
ideas or arguments presented in the main body are summarised and set out in  the 
conclusion. Some essays require that you state an opinion, i.e. come to a conclusion . 
Summarise the main arguments which support your view, not the ones that weaken your 
judgement. Make it clear what implications flow from your conclusion. While the 
introduction and the conclusion should agree with each other and never be contradictory, 
avoid the temptation to repeat the introduction or introduce new information. The 
conclusion is normally about the same length as or shorter than the introduction.  
Short Answer Questions (SAQs) 
 
SAQs are similar to essay-type questions as they also consist of a statement and/or query 
on a topic together with a prompt. They are often used in examinations to expand the 
number of topics that can be tested in one section or paper.  Although they can be treated 
like mini-essays, the expected response is usually only one or two paragraphs long or 
might be set out as a list of items.  
The following approach to an SAQ should be taken. 
1. Begin by defining all the terms in the question and take note of any boundaries 
or parameters given e.g. if you are asked for two particulars, Don’t write out five 
hoping you get it right. Add supporting examples only if requested. 
2. Write the answer in simple concise sentences including only as much 
information as possible - this is more important than style. 
If you are asked to answer within a specific number of words, make sure that is 
what you do.
